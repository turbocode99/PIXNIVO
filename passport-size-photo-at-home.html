How to Make a Passport Size Photo at Home (Official Dimensions & Guidelines)

31 May 2026•Updated 23 October 2026•6 min read

A passport photo is one of the few pictures where "nice" doesn't matter and "correct" is everything. Passport offices check size, framing, background and lighting against fixed rules, and a photo that fails any of them delays your application. The good news: with a phone, a plain wall and a window, you can take a compliant photo at home in about fifteen minutes.

This guide covers the sizes most countries use, how to shoot and crop the photo, what to watch for when printing or uploading, and the mistakes that cause most rejections.

Always check your issuing authority first. Rules change and differ by country. The figures below are a practical starting point at the time of writing; the official passport website for your country has the final word.

Common passport photo sizes

Two sizes cover most of the world: 35 × 45 mm, used across Europe, the UK, Australia and much of Asia and Africa, and the 2 × 2 inch square used by the United States. The pixel figures show the size you need for a sharp print at 300 DPI.

Notice that head size is not a fixed percentage everywhere. A US photo leaves more room above the head than a Schengen photo, so cropping a single image "the same way" for different countries will usually get one of them wrong.

Step 1: Set up the shot

  • Background: stand about half a metre in front of a plain white, off-white or light grey wall with no texture, pattern or furniture visible. Standing slightly away from the wall softens your shadow.
  • Light: face a large window during the day, so the light falls evenly on both sides of your face. Avoid overhead ceiling lights, which create dark eye sockets, and direct sunlight, which makes you squint.
  • Camera position: put the phone at eye level, 1–1.5 metres away. Holding the phone close distorts the face — noses look larger and ears disappear. If your phone has a 2× lens, use it and step back.
  • Stability: rest the phone on a shelf or a stack of books and use the timer, or ask someone else to take it. Selfies held at arm's length are usually too close and slightly tilted.

Step 2: Pose by the rules

  • Face the camera directly with shoulders square and head straight — not tilted or turned.
  • Keep a neutral expression with your mouth closed and both eyes open. Some countries accept a natural, closed-mouth smile; a toothy grin is widely rejected.
  • Remove glasses. The United States no longer accepts them at all, and glare or reflections are a common reason for rejection elsewhere.
  • Keep hair away from your eyes and the edges of your face. Hats and head coverings are generally only allowed for religious or medical reasons, with the full face visible.
  • Wear everyday clothing that contrasts with the background. A white shirt against a white wall can make your head look like it's floating.

Take a dozen shots. Small differences in expression and head angle are easier to judge on a laptop screen than on your phone.

Step 3: Crop to the right size

Pick the sharpest, most evenly lit photo and crop it to your country's proportions with your head at the required size.

  • For the US 2 × 2 inch square: open the photo in PIXNIVO's Photo Editor, choose the 1:1 crop and export as JPG. Your head should take up roughly half to two-thirds of the height, with a little space above the hair.
  • For 35 × 45 mm or 50 × 70 mm: these use 7:9 and 5:7 proportions, which aren't among the editor's presets. Use a crop tool that accepts a custom ratio — most phone photo editors and desktop editors do — or the passport template offered by your photo lab.

Keep the edit to cropping. Small corrections such as straightening are normally fine, but filters, skin smoothing and retouching are not — several authorities, including the US Department of State, reject photos that look digitally altered.

A note on replacing the background: PIXNIVO's AI Background Remover can swap a busy wall for plain white or light grey, which is useful for ID cards, membership cards and job applications. For passports, check your issuing authority's rules first: some treat any background replacement as an alteration. Shooting against a genuinely plain wall is the safer route.

Step 4: Print or upload

Printing

Don't print passport photos on plain office paper. Order a standard 6 × 4 inch (10 × 15 cm) photo print from a lab, pharmacy kiosk or online service; most offer a passport or ID template that fits several copies on one sheet. Check the printed size with a ruler before you submit it — some printers quietly scale images to fit the page.

Uploading online

Many countries now accept digital photos for online applications, often with their own pixel and file-size rules. The US online renewal, for example, asks for a square image between 600 × 600 and 1,200 × 1,200 pixels; the UK asks for at least 600 × 750 pixels. If a portal sets a maximum file size, use the Image Compressor to bring the JPG under it without visible loss. How to reduce photo size for online forms walks through hitting exact limits.

Canada is an exception worth knowing: printed Canadian passport photos must carry the photographer's details and the date on the back, so they generally need to come from a commercial photographer.

Frequently asked questions

Can I take my passport photo with a phone?

In most countries, yes, as long as the result meets the size, lighting and background rules. A modern phone camera has far more resolution than a passport photo needs. Exceptions are countries like Canada that require a photographer's stamp on printed photos.

What resolution should the file be?

For printing, aim for at least 300 DPI at the final size — 600 × 600 px for a 2 × 2 inch photo or 413 × 531 px for 35 × 45 mm. Starting from a full-resolution phone photo gives you plenty of room to crop. For online uploads, follow the portal's pixel range exactly.

How do I take a passport photo of a baby?

Lay the baby on a plain white sheet and photograph from directly above, or sit them in a car seat covered with a white sheet. Most authorities relax the expression and eyes-open rules for babies, but no hands, toys or dummies should be visible.
