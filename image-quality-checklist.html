The Image Quality Checklist: 7 Steps That Fix Most Problems

5 August 2026•Updated 23 October 2026•5 min read

Most image problems come down to the same handful of mistakes, made in the same order. Once you know what they are, they're easy to avoid — and the difference between a photo that gets accepted, printed or clicked and one that doesn't is often a single setting.

This is the checklist, in the order that actually matters. Each step explains what to do, why it matters, and how to do it with PIXNIVO's free in-browser tools.

1. Start from the best original you have

Everything else depends on this. Each time an image is saved as JPG, compressed, or sent through a messaging app, a little quality is permanently lost — and the losses stack.

  • Work from the file in your camera roll, not the version a friend sent back to you.
  • Avoid screenshotting an image to "get a copy" — that locks in your screen's resolution and adds another round of compression.
  • Never edit and re-save the same JPG over and over; keep the original and export copies.
  • If the image came from a website or a designer, ask for the original file rather than saving the version shown on the page.

A photo that has been through WhatsApp twice can't be restored, no matter what you do to it afterwards. How to compress images for WhatsApp explains how to avoid that in the first place.

2. Do the destructive steps in the right order

Some operations throw information away. Doing them in the wrong sequence throws away more than necessary. The correct order:

  1. Crop — remove what you don't need, so later steps spend their effort on what remains.
  2. Edit — brightness, contrast and colour, while you still have every pixel to work with.
  3. Resize — set the final dimensions for the destination.
  4. Compress — choose the format and quality last, once.

Compressing first and cropping afterwards means you compressed pixels you were about to delete — and then compressed again. Resizing before editing means your adjustments work on fewer pixels than you had. In PIXNIVO, crop and adjust in the Photo Editor and export as PNG, then resize and compress in the Image Compressor. Exporting the middle step as PNG avoids an extra round of lossy compression.

3. Match the dimensions to the actual use

The most common waste is an image far larger than anything that will display it. A 4,000-pixel photo shown as a 600-pixel card on a website is downloaded at full size by every visitor.

4. Pick the format on purpose

  • Photographs → JPG
  • Logos, graphics, screenshots of text, anything with transparency → PNG
  • Images for your own website → WebP
  • Uploads to a bank, government portal or job site → JPG, unless told otherwise

Saving photographs as PNG is the most expensive mistake: it creates a file several times larger for a difference nobody can see. The full reasoning is in JPG vs PNG vs WebP.

5. Compress to a target, not out of habit

Work backwards from the requirement:

  • No limit specified → 80–85% quality is visually indistinguishable from the original for almost every photo.
  • A stated file-size limit → reduce the dimensions first, then lower quality only as far as needed. See how to hit exact KB limits.
  • Will be printed → 90% or higher, and don't reduce dimensions below what the print size needs.

The Image Compressor's preview shows the real compressed result and its exact size, so you can stop at the lowest setting that still looks right instead of guessing.

6. Check it at the size it will be seen

Reviewing a photo full-screen when it will appear as a small 200-pixel card tells you very little. Check it in context:

  • Product photo? Look at it as a thumbnail. If you can't tell what it is at a glance, crop tighter.
  • Printing it? Zoom to 100% and look at the finest detail you care about.
  • Uploading an ID photo? Zoom in on the face and check it's sharp and evenly lit.
  • Cut out a background? Zoom into the edges and look for leftover halos.
  • Text in the image? Make sure the smallest text is readable on a phone screen.

7. Keep your originals

Always export copies and leave the original file untouched. If a portal rejects the photo, a client wants a different crop, or you need to print it later, you can start again from full quality instead of from a compressed copy.

A good habit: save edited exports in a separate folder from your originals, and add the purpose to the file name — passport-600px.jpg, product-2000px.jpg — so you never overwrite the master by accident.

Two details that are easy to miss

Colour that shifts between devices

Photos from cameras and design software sometimes use wide-gamut colour profiles. Shown in a browser or on another device, colours can look duller or more saturated than you expected. For anything going on the web or into a portal, standard sRGB colour gives the most consistent results. Images exported from a browser canvas, as PIXNIVO's are, are saved in sRGB.

Hidden information in the file

Photos carry EXIF metadata: the camera model, the date and often the exact GPS location. Before posting a photo publicly or sending it to strangers, consider removing it. Re-saving a photo through the Image Compressor creates a clean copy containing only the pixels.

Frequently asked questions

Should I sharpen my photos?

Only lightly, and only at the end, after resizing. Sharpening before you shrink an image is mostly wasted, and sharpening after heavy compression exaggerates the artifacts. If an image needs enlarging, the Image Upscaler includes sharpening tuned for that.

Does editing in the browser reduce quality?

Not if you follow the order above. Adjusting and exporting as PNG is lossless; the only lossy step is the final JPG or WebP export, which happens once.

Are my images uploaded when I use these tools?

No. The Photo Editor, Image Compressor, Background Remover and Image Upscaler all run in your browser, on your device.
