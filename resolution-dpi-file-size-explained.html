Resolution, DPI & File Size Explained in Plain English

24 May 2026•Updated 23 October 2026•5 min read

"Please upload a high-resolution image, 300 DPI, under 2 MB." Instructions like this mix three different ideas — pixels, print density and file size — as if they were the same thing. They aren't, and once you can tell them apart, most image requirements stop being confusing.

This guide explains each term in plain English, shows the arithmetic that connects them, and answers the questions that come up most often.

Pixels: the only thing a digital image really has

A digital image is a grid of coloured squares called pixels. A photo described as 4,000 × 3,000 is 4,000 pixels wide and 3,000 tall — 12 million pixels, or 12 megapixels (MP). That grid is the image. Everything else is either a description of how to print it or a measure of how much storage it takes.

On a screen, only the pixel grid matters. A 1,920-pixel-wide image fills a Full HD monitor whether its file says 72 DPI, 300 DPI or nothing at all.

"Resolution" is used loosely for both the pixel dimensions and the print density. When someone asks for "high resolution", they almost always mean "enough pixels".

DPI and PPI: how pixels become inches

PPI (pixels per inch) describes how tightly an image's pixels are packed when it is printed. DPI (dots per inch) technically describes the ink dots a printer lays down, but in everyday use the two are treated as the same thing, and most forms say DPI when they mean PPI.

The one formula worth remembering:
Print size (inches) = pixels ÷ PPI
A 3,000-pixel-wide image printed at 300 PPI is 3,000 ÷ 300 = 10 inches wide. Printed at 150 PPI, the same pixels stretch to 20 inches — bigger, but each pixel twice as large.

The DPI tag doesn't change the picture

Image files can carry a DPI value in their metadata, but it is only a suggestion for printing. Changing it from 72 to 300 doesn't add a single pixel or make the photo any sharper; it just tells printing software to make the print smaller. Two files with the same pixels and different DPI tags look identical on screen.

That is also why "72 DPI" doesn't mean low quality. A 6,000-pixel-wide photo tagged at 72 DPI contains exactly the same detail as the same photo tagged at 300.

How big can you print?

For prints viewed at arm's length — photos, flyers, magazines — 300 PPI looks sharp. Larger prints are viewed from further away, so they can use lower densities without looking soft.

A typical 12 MP phone photo covers everything up to A4 at full 300 PPI and makes a good A3 print at around 250 PPI. Posters and banners viewed from across a room are commonly printed at 100–150 PPI.

Screens: why websites use larger images than the space they fill

Many phones and laptops have high-density displays that use two or three physical pixels for every pixel a web page describes. An image shown in a 600-pixel-wide space on such a screen looks crisp only if the file is around 1,200 pixels wide. That's why sensible web image widths run from about 1,200 to 2,000 pixels, even though the layout itself is narrower.

File size: megapixels are not megabytes

Megapixels count pixels; megabytes measure storage. They're connected, but compression sits in between.

  • Uncompressed, each pixel takes 3 bytes (one each for red, green and blue). A 12 MP photo is therefore about 36 MB of raw image data.
  • As a JPG at 80% quality, the same photo is usually 2–4 MB — roughly a tenth of the raw size — with no visible difference.
  • As a PNG, which is lossless, a photograph often stays at 10–20 MB.

The content of the picture matters too. Smooth areas such as sky or a plain wall compress very well; fine detail such as foliage, gravel or image noise doesn't. Two photos with identical dimensions and quality settings can differ in file size by a factor of three.

KB vs KiB: Strictly, 1 KB is 1,000 bytes, but many systems — including Windows — count 1,024 bytes as a kilobyte. When a portal limit is tight, aim about 5% under it so the difference can't trip you up.

Putting it together: reading a typical requirement

Take "2 × 2 inches at 300 DPI, under 240 KB". Translated:

  1. Pixels: 2 in × 300 = 600, so the image should be 600 × 600 pixels.
  2. File size: a 600 × 600 JPG at 80–90% quality is usually 60–150 KB, comfortably under the limit.
  3. DPI tag: usually irrelevant once the pixels are right. Images exported from a browser, including PIXNIVO, are typically saved without a meaningful DPI value, and most programs then display 72 or 96.

Frequently asked questions

Can I make a print sharper by increasing the DPI?

Not by changing the number alone. Raising the DPI tag only makes the print smaller. To print larger and sharp, you need more pixels — ideally from the original camera file. Upscaling helps a little; see how to upscale without blur for what it can and can't do.

My photo is 5 MB. Is it high resolution?

Not necessarily. File size depends on compression and content as well as pixels. Check the pixel dimensions instead — on most computers they're shown in the file's properties or details panel.

What resolution should I use for a website?

Around 1,200–2,000 pixels wide for full-width images and roughly twice the displayed width for smaller ones, saved as WebP or JPG at 75–85% quality. JPG vs PNG vs WebP helps you choose the format.

Why is my scanned document so large?

Scanners often default to 300 or 600 DPI in full colour, which produces a large pixel grid for every page. For documents meant for screen reading, 150 DPI is usually enough — see how to compress a PDF that's too large.
